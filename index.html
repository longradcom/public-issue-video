01 / Executive investment thesis

The market is real. The wedge is not yet proven.

The original commercial idea is attractive but faces a brutal price spectrum: minutes-to-video DIY software at a few dollars per month, a domain specialist at £39 per video, and studio-produced explainers from about £2,000. The product must solve a buyer problem beyond rendering footage.

01 • BUYER PROBLEM

Communications teams need to translate expertise into understandable media.

Reports, regulatory changes, science, public programmes and policy developments require scripts, visual interpretation, brand control and approval—not just footage.

Relevant survey signal: 72% of responding nonprofits make short vertical video; only 24% of video creators produce programme explainers [S04].

02 • PRODUCT HYPOTHESIS

From approved sources to a useful, finished explainer.

Upload a document, URL or brief → choose intended use → approve an evidence-linked script → receive professional EN/ES formats with captions, versions and clear rights.

Must outperform inexpensive alternatives on quality of interpretation and institutional usability.

03 • COMMERCIAL HYPOTHESIS

Two routes to market, one repeatable software workflow.

Sell direct to organisations with existing video expenditure; also test PR/public-affairs agencies as buyers and white-label resellers. Reserve human service for justified accounts.

Do not assume that $5 generation cost means $5 fully loaded delivery.

Thesis to test: A research or policy communications team will pay an institution-grade price for a reliable, branded, sourced, English/Spanish explainer delivered quickly—even though cheaper DIY options exist. Counter-hypothesis: buyers treat it as another video generator and will not pay enough to support acquisition and review costs.
Product focus

Start with one clear video-production problem

Build and validate a specialist, increasingly self-service service for short public-information, research and issue explainers. The initial test should demonstrate repeatable production quality, a buyer-led ordering process and viable acquisition economics.

02 / Market evidence

Large universe; sharply smaller buyer pool.

Organisational counts should never be multiplied directly by a hypothetical video spend and called a realistic TAM. Buyers need a communications function, funding, recurring needs and a purchasing process.

From a 322-person respondent survey, not a random representative sample of all registered nonprofits. It measures creation, not willingness to outsource. [S03–04]

Why demand exists

  • Video is a normal communications output, but teams face constrained time and budgets [S03, S09].
  • Institutional reports and research provide source material suitable for structured script development.
  • Organisations often need the same idea in multiple formats, languages and revisions.
  • Evidence of premium demand: bespoke research/nonprofit explainers are commissioned from studios [S23–25].

Why broad TAM claims fail

  • Many charities have no paid communications staff.
  • Universities, research nonprofits and charities overlap—adding their counts double-counts.
  • “Produces video” is not equivalent to “buys external video”.
  • Organisational procurement and approvals can dominate the conversion cycle.
  • Institution budgets and video frequency require buyer-level verification.

04 / Competitor landscape

The competitors are both cheaper and more sophisticated than expected.

Nineteen verified/partially verified vendors grouped by actual substitution pattern: document-to-video specialist, horizontal AI creator tools, explainer software, traditional studios, and public-affairs/nonprofit agencies. We compare what buyers receive—not incomparable seat fees against a finished video.

Direct specialist

Explain My Charity

£39/video + VAT, up to 5 min and 3 render/revision rounds; from £399/mo for 15 + VAT. Human review +£99/video. [S01]

Crucially: document input, script approval and fast rendering are already offered. Requires differentiated storytelling/buyer segment or higher-touch assurance.

DIY software

Pictory / HeyGen / Visla

Public plans around $18–$29/month for some entry products, depending on billing. Some support document/script/video and localisation. [S10–11, S19]

These anchor production-tool cost, but still require operator skills and editorial review.

Full-service studio

Wyzowl / Demo Duck

Wyzowl publicly starts at £2,000 ($2,700) for a custom animated explainer; Demo Duck has documented research-institute work and puts higher-end animated explainers at $20,000–$25,000. [S23, S25, S35]

Premium buyers pay for idea, clarity, bespoke design, human QA and client service.

Substitute-test: the real battle

Against £39: can we demonstrate visibly better script interpretation, stronger visual treatment, EN/ES localisation, institution-grade review, and recurring workflow value?

Against $25/mo SaaS: can we remove enough editor time to justify a managed premium?

Against £2,000 bespoke: can we preserve trust and quality while materially reducing approval time and cost?

Research quality and caveats

Direct = product and/or price confirmed on primary vendor material; Partial = historical or third-party page. “Not documented” does not mean a feature is absent.

All figures are vendor list prices where visible, not quotes obtained. Per-seat AI SaaS, rendered-minute credits, and fully serviced video quotes cannot be compared as equivalent units. Check dates and terms before customer-facing use.

05 / Defensible positioning

The intersection is the product—not the video model.

There is already extensive overlap between generic content-generation tools and studio workflows. The defendable proposition, if validated, is the domain-specific buyer journey and repeatable editorial operating system for public issues.

Horizontal software

Cheap creator seats, prompting, avatars, captions, localised video. Pictory, HeyGen, Visla, Lumen5, Synthesia.

Traditional creative studios

Original concepts, human editors, scripts, high polish, longer turnaround. Wyzowl, Demo Duck.

Public-affairs / NGO agencies

Policy knowledge, clients, stakeholder relationships, strategy and approvals. Blue State, Social Driver, Big Duck.

Public Issue Video (proposed)

Source → interpret → institutional script approval → reliable short video → localise → deliver/reorder, under one repeatable guided workflow. Need proof buyer values this above DIY and specialist £39 competitors.

VALUE TO PROVE

Specialised editorial interpretation

Turn a technical report into a visually comprehensible 60-second narrative without changing facts. Source traceability and explicit unsupported-claim flags.

VALUE TO PROVE

Institution-grade approvals

Draft script + corrections, one accountable approver, version history, rights/captions, clear publishing permissions. Already partly offered by competitors; test quality and convenience.

VALUE TO PROVE

Repeatability & distribution

Multi-format EN/ES video packs, brand memory, templates and low-touch onboarding, or agency white-label. Lock-in from reliable process—not the underlying model.

Anti-USP: “AI turns your PDF into a video in minutes” is already the positioning of existing products, including a cheap domain specialist. “We use the newest model” and “we support Spanish” are not durable differentiators by themselves.
06 / Pricing & unit economics

Price the finished problem solved, not model tokens.

Offer design and price points below are experiments—not observed willingness to pay. Show a quote, scope, approval and revision policy. Only promise free rendered trials when the unit cost, quality and anti-abuse measures are proven.

Offer A — self-service

One issue video

Indicative test: $149 / $399

45–90 sec; source/brief upload; AI-guided script with approval; captions; EN or US Spanish; selected visual style; 1 correction window.

A/B test price, do not automatically position $399 as established value.

Offer B — repeat buyer

Institution content pack

Indicative test: 4 videos for $999

Consistent branded series, multiple source documents, approval queue, EN/ES options, scheduled/pooled usage.

Subscription vs prepaid pack to be tested; buyer may have variable publication cadence.

Offer C — managed

Agency / enterprise

Custom scope and monthly minimum

White-label production, special reviews, additional styles, volume SLA, dedicated support, legal/security checks, invoicing and approvals.

Human calls should depend on credible order value, not every $199 enquiry.

At lower prices, sales and review costs can erase the profit even if generation costs $5. Add repeat orders and account-management cost in a cohort model before raising funds.

Three different buyer journeys

  1. Card-first: short brief → script preview → purchase → human/automated QC → delivery.
  2. AI-assisted: enquiry/chat → generated proposal and timeline → approval → card/invoice.
  3. Managed: qualification → person if needed → quote/PO → proof + SLA.

Randomise by account, not by time alone; weekly sequential prices are confounded by market and lead mix.

Trial and subscription design

Test credit-card validation + trial versus paid pilot with satisfaction guarantee and no-card script preview. Clearly disclose billing date and cancellation. Institutional buyers may request invoices/POs rather than cards.

Do not assume card-required free trials improve trust or legal compliance; measure fraudulent usage, qualification and activation separately.

07 / Go-to-market experiments

A ten-day sprint to reduce uncertainty—not magically close 30 institutions.

The launch should test channel, vertical, message, price and delivery performance in controlled cells. The first 10 days can establish interest and early orders; procurement-heavy customers often take longer to pay.

DAYS 0–2

Build the baseline

Pick 2 direct niches + 1 agency cohort, 100 prospects each. Review existing published video output, budget signs and named buyer roles. Produce 12–24 clearly labelled sample videos (not 500).

DAYS 3–5

Run controlled offers

3 landing-page claims: speed, explanation quality, source/review confidence. Test 2 offers, 2 formats; track interest by segment and channel.

DAYS 6–10

Interview and fulfil

Compare direct outbound vs agency introductions vs paid service discovery. Offer optional short demos, gather objections, deliver first paid orders if possible.

DAYS 11–30

Observe conversion

Follow purchasing approvals, reorder cadence and refund/revision rates. Scale only the segment with evidence of repeatable buyer economics.

Stage gates to agree in the team meeting

Day 10: qualified demand exists beyond views and likes; interviews uncover recurring use cases.

Day 30: a repeatable order flow, positive contribution on delivered work and at least one second purchase/volume indication.

Day 60: enough cohort evidence to decide to scale, change niche or stop. 30 paying customers is an ambition, not a statistically grounded 10-day forecast.

08 / Product architecture & launch specification

A guided editorial workflow, not a toolbox.

01 • SOURCEBring the evidenceUpload PDF, research link, transcript or issue brief. Collect rights, audience intent and publication status.
02 • INTERPRETDraft the angleAI extracts claims and proposes 1–3 short approaches with traceable source notes.
03 • APPROVESign off scriptClient edits words, facts, brand, voice and language before expensive rendering.
04 • PRODUCERender the videoTemplate-based motion graphics, B-roll, charts, voice and subtitles. Log cost & failed shots.
05 • QCCheck the outputVisual accuracy, citations/claims, subtitles, pronunciation, image rights and AI labels where applicable.
06 • DELIVERPack and learn9:16 first, English/Spanish options, export, revisions, simple feedback and re-order.

Build for v0

  • Organisation account and minimal brand kit.
  • Input document/URL/brief with source tracking.
  • Guided script preview and approval; internal fact flags.
  • Two reliably reproducible styles, not nine.
  • One finished 45–90 sec master + captions.
  • EN and US Spanish, with human spot-check as needed.
  • Per-job production cost and revision logging.
  • Invoice/card route and explicit delivery time.

Do not build for v0

  • Full social scheduling and media buying.
  • Unlimited stylistic control or infinite free revisions.
  • Live automatic monitoring of every public issue.
  • Political campaign microtargeting.
  • Full custom video editor or proprietary video model.
  • 100 fully automated sample videos before there is buyer validation.
  • All verticals and languages simultaneously.
  • Untested integrations or partnership/equity assumptions.

Proposed website: four concise buyer pages

Homepage
“Turn complex public issues into videos people understand.”

For research teams
Paper/report → sourced narrative → public video.

For associations
Member updates, regulatory explainers and series.

For agencies
White-label production, brand and approval controls.

All routes should share the same two-minute intake: source/topic → objective → draft script preview → select format/language → approve → pay / request PO.

09 / Critical risks and decision log

What could make this venture fail?

Commodity collapseRender cost drops further, customers use existing tools. Counter-test: editorial value and time saved vs £39/$25 DIY benchmark.
Buyer procurement frictionOrganisations need multiple approvals for small orders. Counter-test: quote/PO, AI-assisted sales and channel partner route.
Quality and misinformationResearch, numbers, complex policy or public health are misrepresented. Trace source claims; show a human-approval path and clear disclosures.
Agency channel conflictResellers fear their customers being taken. Define non-solicitation, white-labeling, lead ownership and pilot scope in writing.
Misread demandLikes on speculative demos do not equal a purchase. Measure real qualified briefs, paid trials, reorders and decision-makers.
Regulated contentUS 501(c)(3) political campaign restrictions, FEC rules for election work, sensitive medical claims and public-sector accessibility add scope-dependent obligations [S29–31].

Decisions for the Public Issue Video pilot

  1. Agree who funds the experiment, who owns pilot-created assets, and who manages operations during the test.
  2. Select one direct niche and one channel (agency) to test first; use the scoring table as an input, not a verdict.
  3. Set two creative styles, EN/US-ES support and one 45–90 sec standard deliverable.
  4. Choose the initial $ price bands and specific revision/service scope; agree gross-margin floor.
  5. Decide responsibility for sample creation, QA, demand capture and customer conversations.
  6. Agree stage-gates and a limited experiment budget before promising 100–500 speculative videos.

Legal detail: the IRS forbids 501(c)(3)s from intervening for or against candidates; public issue education may be permissible when conducted nonpartisanly [S29]. Foreign nationals have special restrictions regarding election-related decision-making under FEC rules [S30]. A US company alone does not erase these restrictions. This is scope guidance, not legal advice.
